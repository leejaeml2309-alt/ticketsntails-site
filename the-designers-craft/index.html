Special Founder Dossier · The Designer's Eye & The Builder's Hands

The Designer's Craft

A refined house shared with dogs and cats asks the same discipline as a high-traffic public room: durable materials, effortless upkeep, fluid circulation, acoustics, warm light, and furniture made for the life that will actually take place there.

The rooms we keep for dogs and cats are often treated as a lesser problem: a cover, a mat, a corner set aside. They are not. They are a public problem in miniature. A hall that must take daily traffic, a floor that must be cleaned without ceremony, a path that must remain clear, a light that must be kind at the end of the day, and furniture that can be used hard without looking as though it was designed for a kennel — these are the same questions that govern a café. The designer's eye and the builder's hands belong together in both.

This special founder dossier sets out that argument through a French Beaux-Arts training — in Chalon-sur-Saône and at ENSBA Dijon — and through two interiors in South Korea that Marc, the founder of Pawpartout, designed and built entirely alone: Café Bonjour Madame and Maison Mirabelle, both in Seoul. The photographs here are of those rooms, taken as they were used. Nothing in them was generated.

A builder's thesis

What those two cafés in South Korea make plain is that an extraordinary interior does not require an extravagant luxury budget. A tight budget, modest and accessible materials, and practical builder's common sense — paired with a Beaux-Arts-trained eye — can still produce a room of real distinction.

An artistic and academic journey

The work begins in Chalon-sur-Saône. Marc spent around ten years in Wednesday fine-arts classes at the École Municipale d'Art de Chalon-sur-Saône, and then a one-year Beaux-Arts preparatory program at the same school — a broad grounding in film photography, graphic design, web design, life drawing and figure studies, watercolor, sculpture, and ceramics. He then spent two years in hydraulic-press printmaking and engraving in Chalon-sur-Saône, working alongside people with mental disabilities.

He later entered the École nationale supérieure d'art de Dijon (ENSBA Dijon) in the DNAP Spatial Design program (Design d'espace). He continued through year two and discontinued the course in that year for family reasons; he does not hold the diploma. As early as year one, he was selected above his level for two upper-year workshops normally closed to first-years: an architecture workshop on la Canopée with a prominent architect, in collaboration with the Marseille School of Architecture; and a DNAP / DNSEP workshop with the Mainz School of Architecture, hosted at ENSBA Dijon.

Alongside this training came a practice of running and making. He has managed businesses since the age of twenty-three, and taught French for Beaux-Arts entrance preparation in South Korea. The contrast is the point: a formation received in France was then tested in South Korea, where he designed and built both Café Bonjour Madame and Maison Mirabelle entirely alone, from start to finish, in Seoul. The case studies that follow are those two rooms.

Case study 1 — Café Bonjour Madame, Seoul, 2012–2014

The interior of Café Bonjour Madame was designed and built entirely alone, in two months, with his own materials. It is a study in spatial layout, in finishes that could take daily foot traffic, in warm light, and in a tactile register of wood, plaster, and glass — a public room asked to work every day and still feel composed.

A house for dogs and cats can learn directly from a room like this. Circulation has to be obvious. Surfaces have to wipe clean without losing their warmth. Light has to be low enough to be kind and strong enough to work by. Nothing can be too fragile to touch. These are not hospitality tricks; they are the ordinary ethics of a well-built interior.

Case study 2 — Maison Mirabelle, Yeonnam-dong, Seoul, 2019–2023

Maison Mirabelle was an artisanal handmade cake house in Yeonnam-dong. Marc designed the interior and built it alone in three months. The space had no plumbing; he installed all of it himself, and coordinated a professional electrician for the electrical work. The photographs record a pale, daylight room: a long table, a service counter, hanging plants, and painted cabinetry made to be used.

A cake house is not a kennel, and a house shared with animals is not a café. What they share is the demand for a room that can be built, cleaned, and lived in without apology. Durable materials, clear circulation, quiet acoustics, warm light, and furniture made for the work of the day — this is the same brief that will govern the forthcoming 3D Pet-Interior Studio, where a room can be laid out with custom dog-and-cat furniture and tested against the finishes it will actually have to bear.

The two rooms in Seoul are also a proof. A tight budget, modest and accessible materials, and a builder's common sense, held to the standard of a Beaux-Arts-trained eye, can still yield an extraordinary result. The designer's eye without the builder's hands is a drawing. The builder's hands without the designer's eye are only a fit-out. The rooms we owe to the animals we live with require both.
